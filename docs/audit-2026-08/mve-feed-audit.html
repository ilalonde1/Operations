The finding

Schema is not data

Every one of these feeds looks usable from its field list. Six of them are not, and in each case the failure is invisible until you query the contents.

This matters because it is precisely the wall a business-development team hits when it tries to build lead generation from public records. The portal advertises project names, unit counts and valuations; the field list confirms them; and then the field turns out to be empty, or frozen three years ago, or stamped identically across forty child permits. None of that is discoverable from documentation. It only shows up when you count rows.

✕ Two feeds actively mislead

Dallas publishes a building-permit dataset of 126,840 rows whose portal metadata claims a June 2026 update. Queried by year: 52,037 rows in 2019, 27,459 in 2020, and zero in every year since. Five years dead, advertised as current.

Charlotte’s “Committed Development Entitlement Update” is the single richest schema found anywhere — development name, address, acreage, single-family and multifamily unit counts, hotel rooms, and square footage split by use. It holds 1,012 rows. The newest approval in it is 23 May 2022. The metadata says 2023.

The trap nobody documents

The unit-count field is the least reliable field in every feed

It is also the one a business-development team would naturally filter on, which is what makes it dangerous.

  • Raleigh carries housingunitstotal, and it is well populated historically — 115,401 rows overall, 935 of them with 40 or more units. In 2026 the feed issued 3,700 permits and exactly 2 of them carry a unit count. The newest permit anywhere in the file with 40+ units is 14 July 2025. Every other field kept working: cost 3,700 of 3,700, contractor 3,669, owner 3,639, project name 3,306. One field went dark and nothing announced it. Filter on units and you would conclude Raleigh stopped building apartments last summer.
  • Henderson carries MultiFamilyDwellingUnits. It is populated on 19 rows out of 28,137. I previously described this feed as richer than San Diego’s on the strength of its field list — that was wrong, and reading the contents corrected it.
  • Austin populates units properly, but the number describes the host building, not the permit’s scope: a lobby fit-out in a 30-storey tower reports 35 units. And its valuation is the whole project’s, repeated on every child permit — one lakefront development stamped $830M onto 81 separate permits for pergolas, pools and parking garages.

▲ The workaround, and it is simple

Filter on valuation and contractor, never on unit count. Those two fields are populated and current in every live feed tested. Then dedupe by address, because several jurisdictions issue many permits per project and repeat the project-level value on each.

A correction to this document

Testing one source is not auditing the jurisdiction

The first pass tested Phoenix's open-data portal, found 23 rows of yearly totals, and wrote the city off. That verdict was wrong, and it was wrong in the same shape as everything else here — a conclusion drawn from one artefact rather than from the jurisdiction.

Phoenix runs a separate map service, maps.phoenix.gov/pub/rest/services/Public/Planning_Permit, carrying 68,292 permits and 13,022 plan reviews, row-level, with project name, address, type, status and dates — current to 27 August 2026, the day of this audit. Among its 80 case types is FINAL SITE PLAN – MAJOR, which is the submittal whose plan set carries the full consultant directory. Recent examples resolve by name: Momentum Apartments, Senior Housing at 41st Ave, 17th St Apartments, Mayo Clinic Patient Pavilion, Scannell Roeser 24 Industrial Park.

✕ Why the first pass missed it

The service sits at /pub/rest/services. Every probe of the conventional /arcgis/rest/services path returned 404, and a 404 against a guessed host proves nothing at all. The endpoint was found by searching the ArcGIS Online organisation catalogue for the city's published services — derived, not guessed. That rule now applies to every endpoint and every firm URL in this work.

▲ And the field that looks like the prize is not

PROFESS_NAME is populated on 90.6% of plan reviews, and its name promises the design professional — the one thing no feed anywhere carries. Read the values and it is the licensed contractor: the most common entries are TO BE BID, OWNER, Valley Fire Sprinkler, solar installers, Sundt, Austin Commercial, TSMC. Useful for who builds in Phoenix. It is not an architect and must never be presented as one.

Scottsdale tells the mirror-image story. Its Active_CDS_Cases service is immaculate — 82 cases, every one of eight fields populated on every row, applicant included. And the newest submittal is 25 February 2026, because the city moved to a new portal (SPUR) in January and the map only covers what preceded it. The live successor exposes a search API that requires an OAuth token, so it is reachable by a browser and not by us. A perfect feed can still be a closed one.

What would extend this

The blockers are technical, not analytical

  • A browser-rendering fetch capability is the single highest-value addition. Three separate verifications died on it today: Austin’s permit-detail portal returns a 958-byte session shell, Charlotte’s own rezoning PDFs sit behind an Akamai bot wall that Google’s crawler passes and we do not, and Thornton Tomasetti’s project page returns 403. Each holds data we can see exists.
  • BLDUP.com maintains exactly the schema this exercise is reconstructing by hand — developer, architect, general contractor, structural engineer, landscape — and masks every value behind a paid seat.
  • BEX (AZBEX / NVBEX / DATABEX) covers Arizona and Nevada from one publisher with full design teams, and DATABEX indexes every Arizona job over $5M. Quote-only pricing.
  • OAuth-gated portals are the newer wall. Scottsdale’s live SPUR search API exists and answers — a GET returns 405, so the method is right — but every anonymous POST returns 500 because it wants a token from Tyler’s identity service. Cities are migrating onto these platforms, so this blocker grows over time while the open ArcGIS ones shrink.
  • Web-search budget was exhausted at 200 calls during this work, which capped the Carolina and Hawaii passes mid-verification. ✔ Resolved — the cap is now 1,000, and both regions have since been completed.
